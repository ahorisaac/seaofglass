History doesn’t repeat, but it rhymes — in incident reports.

every famous breach = one broken promise + one ignored lesson

Study security history and you’ll see the same patterns return: unpatched systems, trusted updates, human trust, and weak segmentation. Below is a working timeline. Filter by attack family, open each event, and read it through the CIA triad: which promise broke, and what should we have done?

12 events

Robert Tappan Morris released a self-replicating worm that spread across the early internet. A reinfection bug made it copy itself repeatedly, slowing thousands of machines to a crawl. It led to the first US felony conviction under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act — and helped inspire the creation of CERT.

The lesson: availability is a security promise too. Self-spreading code needs patching, segmentation and incident coordination.

Mitnick’s intrusions at companies like DEC and Fujitsu relied heavily on social engineering: persuading employees to reveal passwords or grant access. After prison, he became a white-hat consultant — proof that the same curiosity can be redirected with permission and ethics.

The lesson: humans are part of the attack surface. Verify identity, limit disclosure, and train people without blaming them.

A love-letter email attachment spread around the world in hours. The script overwrote files and mailed itself to contacts, causing billions in estimated damage. It wasn’t clever code — it was clever psychology.

The lesson: block risky attachments, filter mail, and teach people that unexpected files are bait, not gifts.

Stuxnet targeted industrial control systems and quietly changed centrifuge behaviour while reporting “all normal.” It demonstrated that malicious code could cause physical damage, even across networks many believed were isolated.

The lesson: air gaps are not magic. Integrity matters in control systems: what the screen says must match what the machine does.

Documents disclosed by Edward Snowden revealed the scale of state-level surveillance programmes and alliances such as Five Eyes. The debate was not only about message content — it was about who could see patterns of communication.

The lesson: metadata (who, when, where) can be as sensitive as content. Privacy is a design decision, not a checkbox.

Attackers entered through credentials linked to a third-party HVAC vendor, then reached payment systems and stole data from tens of millions of payment cards. The front door was strong; the vendor door was not.

The lesson: third parties are your attack surface. Segment vendor access, monitor it, and apply least privilege.

WannaCry used the leaked EternalBlue exploit against unpatched Windows SMB services. Hospitals, factories and businesses were disrupted worldwide. A patch had existed before the outbreak — many systems simply had not applied it.

The lesson: patch known exploited bugs fast. Keep tested backups. Availability can collapse at internet speed.

NotPetya spread through a compromised update mechanism in widely used accounting software, then caused destructive damage far beyond its initial target. It looked like ransomware, but recovery was not the attackers’ real goal.

The lesson: destructive malware can wear a ransomware costume. Updates are powerful — verify their integrity and segment networks.

Attackers exploited a known Apache Struts vulnerability in an Equifax web application. Personal data of about 147 million people was exposed. The bug had a fix; the organisation failed to apply it in time.

The lesson: patch SLAs matter. You cannot protect data you don’t know you have — asset inventories and vulnerability management are not optional.

Attackers compromised the build process of SolarWinds Orion and inserted a backdoor into signed software updates. Thousands of organisations installed the trojanised updates, giving attackers a trusted path into their networks.

The lesson: supply-chain trust must be verified. Monitor vendor behaviour, restrict what management tools can reach, and hunt for anomalous outbound traffic.

A critical flaw in the ubiquitous Log4j logging library allowed remote code execution in countless applications. Defenders struggled because many did not know where the library lived inside their own software supply chain.

The lesson: know your dependencies. SBOMs, dependency scanning and emergency patch playbooks turn panic into process.

A zero-day in the MOVEit file-transfer product was exploited at scale, leading to data theft and extortion across many organisations. Attackers did not need to phish every victim — they compromised one product many victims trusted.

The lesson: internet-facing transfer tools are high-value targets. Patch urgently, minimise stored data, and monitor for bulk outbound transfers.

Memory hook

M-S-W-S: Morris → Stuxnet → WannaCry → SolarWinds. “Morris Sent Worms Skyward.” Four anchor events: 1988, 2010, 2017, 2020. Every one taught a lesson the industry keeps relearning.
