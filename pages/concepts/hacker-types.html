The skill is the same. The permission is the difference.

permission + skill = professional · skill − permission = crime

“Hacker” is not an insult. It means someone who understands systems deeply enough to make them do things their designers didn’t expect. What separates a career from a criminal record is authorisation.

White hatpermission · scope · report
Grey hatno permission · mixed intent
Black hatno permission · harm

W White hat — the professional

Has written permission, works inside an agreed scope, and reports findings so they can be fixed. Penetration testers, red teamers and bug-bounty hunters live here.

  • Motto: “Break it legally, report it clearly.”
  • Proof: contract, scope, rules of engagement.

G Grey hat — the boundary crosser

Acts without permission, often claiming good intent — finds a bug, then reports it. Reporting afterwards does not retroactively authorise the intrusion. In most places, it is still unlawful.

  • Motto: “I helped… without being asked.”
  • Danger: good intent is not a legal shield.

B Black hat — the criminal

No permission, and intent to steal, extort, disrupt or spy. Ransomware crews, fraud rings and malicious intruders live here.

  • Motto: “Take first, ask never.”
  • Reality: the tools may look identical to a white hat’s — the law does not care.

Beyond the three hats

Script kiddie

Runs borrowed tools with little understanding. Still dangerous — and still illegal without permission.

Hacktivist

Attacks for political or social causes. Motive doesn’t grant permission; usually black hat legally.

APT / state-sponsored

Funded, patient and targeted. Advanced Persistent Threats play a long game: access, persistence, espionage.

Insider threat

Already has access. Can be malicious or simply careless — the scariest shortcut past the perimeter.

Red, blue and purple

Red team simulates attacks. Blue team defends, detects and responds. Purple team is the feedback loop between them: attack → detect → tune → repeat. Great security programmes run this loop constantly.

The legal line

Before touching any system you don’t own, you need written authorisation, a defined scope, and rules of engagement. Bug-bounty programmes are standing permission with rules — read them before testing.
